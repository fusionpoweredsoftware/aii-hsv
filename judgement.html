How AIs Make a Judgement Call

When an AI decides whether an answer is good, it isn’t weighing evidence the way a person does. It’s predicting text. This page walks through what that means, where a model’s sense of “good” comes from, and why its calls can be useful and still go wrong.

A call is a prediction

Ask a model “Does this answer solve the problem? Yes or no.” and it doesn’t look anything up or run a test. It reads the whole prompt and predicts the most fitting next words. The verdict is whatever text is most likely to follow, given everything it learned in training and everything you put in the prompt.

The pipeline

Situation + criteria› Tokens› Learned patterns› Probabilities› Verdict + reasons

The one idea: every AI judgement is shaped by three things — what the model learned in training, what the prompt asks for, and how the answer is sampled. Change any of them and the call can change.

Yes, no, or maybe

At the moment of the verdict, the model assigns a score to every possible next token. Those scores are turned into probabilities. The model doesn’t “believe” one answer; it holds a spread across all of them, and one gets picked.

Temperature controls how that pick happens. Near zero, the top choice nearly always wins. Higher, the spread flattens and less likely answers start to show up — which is why the same question can get a different call on a second try.

Where “good” comes from

A model has no built-in standards. Its sense of what counts as a good answer is layered on in training.

  • Pretraining Reading huge amounts of human text teaches it how people argue, praise, critique and grade.
  • Instruction tuning Examples of good responses teach it to follow requests like “rate this from 1 to 10”.
  • Preference training (RLHF and similar) People, or other AIs, compare pairs of answers and pick the better one. A reward model learns to predict those picks, and the main model is tuned to score well on it. In other words, AI judges were trained by judges.
  • Written principles Some labs also train against a written set of principles (a “constitution”), so the model learns to critique answers against stated rules.

Whatever the raters rewarded — including their blind spots — becomes part of the model’s taste.

The prompt sets the standard

The same model makes very different calls depending on how the question is framed.

Three ways to judge

  • Pointwise Score one answer on its own (“rate 1–10”).
  • Pairwise Pick the better of two answers. Usually more consistent than scores.
  • Reference-based Compare against a known good answer.

What sharpens a call

  • A rubric Spell out what earns credit and what doesn’t.
  • Examples Show a few judged cases so it copies the standard.
  • A fixed format Ask for structured output so verdicts can be counted.

A minimal judge prompt

You are grading whether an answer solves a problem.
Problem: {problem}
Answer: {answer}
Criteria: The answer must be practical and directly address the problem.
First explain your reasoning in two sentences, then reply PASS or FAIL.

Reasons first, verdict second

A model writes one token at a time, and each token is conditioned on everything before it. If the verdict comes first, any explanation after it is written to fit a decision already made. If the reasoning comes first, the verdict is conditioned on that reasoning.

That’s why judge prompts often ask the model to think before it answers, and why “reasoning” models that write long internal deliberation tend to judge better on hard cases.

Caveat: written reasoning isn’t guaranteed to be the real reason. Research on chain-of-thought faithfulness has found models giving plausible explanations that leave out what actually drove the answer. Treat the explanation as evidence, not proof.

Known biases of AI judges

These have been measured repeatedly in studies of “LLM-as-a-judge”.

  • Position bias In pairwise comparisons, favoring whichever answer appears first (or second).
  • Length bias Rating longer, more detailed-sounding answers higher, even when they aren’t better.
  • Self-preference Preferring answers written in its own style, or by its own model family.
  • Leniency Giving the benefit of the doubt; preference training rewards agreeable responses.
  • Surface matching Rewarding the right keywords instead of the right idea.
  • Framing and anchoring Swaying toward a verdict the prompt hints at, or toward a score it saw earlier.

Making an AI judge trustworthy

  • Check against people Have humans judge a sample and measure how often the AI agrees.
  • Swap the order In pairwise judging, run both orders and only count consistent verdicts.
  • Ask more than once Sample several verdicts, or use a panel of different models, and take the majority.
  • Check calibration When it says it’s 80% sure, is it right about 80% of the time?
  • Keep it narrow Clear rubric, low temperature, structured output, reasons before verdict.

AI judges are fast, cheap and consistent at scale, which is why they grade benchmarks, filter training data and power reward models. They’re most trustworthy as a measured instrument, not an oracle.

Questions for the room

  • When is a judgement call good enough? Where would you accept an AI’s verdict without a human check, and where never?
  • Judges all the way down If models are trained on AI feedback and graded by AI judges, whose standards are we really measuring?
  • Explanations Does a convincing explanation make you trust a call more than it should?
  • Disagreement When the AI and a person disagree, who should win, and how would you know?
